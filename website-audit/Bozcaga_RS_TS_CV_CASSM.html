Research Statement

Tuğba Bozçağa Lecturer in Politics (Political Methodology), King’s College London tugbabozcaga.github.io | tugba.bozcaga@kcl.ac.uk

My research examines the political economy of development, with an emphasis on service provision and social welfare. My work spans four interconnected areas: (1) state capacity and accountability in public service delivery, with particular attention to ethno-religious heterogeneity and spatial dynamics; (2) the long-term developmental consequences of extractive colonial institutions; (3) the spatial mobility, integration, and climate vulnerability of refugee populations; and (4) the political economy of disasters, especially vulnerability to natural hazards and climate change across urban and rural governance settings. The first pillar is grounded in published and forthcoming work, while the third extends research on refugee welfare to address climate vulnerability. My work on the political economy of disaster resilience, together with research on colonial extraction and its developmental consequences, forms my forward research agenda. These strands underpin grant applications currently in preparation for the European Research Council, the UKRI Future Leaders Fellowship, and the Wellcome Trust.

I study these policy-relevant questions by combining quasi-experimental designs with novel data sources. My commitment to studying causally identified questions in Global South contexts operates under conditions where administrative data are often limited and randomized experimentation is frequently infeasible due to political constraints, instability, or weak state capacity. My focus on quasi-experimental designs and novel data sources reflects a broader commitment to studying understudied, institutionally constrained, and often autocratic or weak-state contexts. Across projects, I employ spatial regression discontinuity designs, difference-in-differences, event studies, panel methods, and spatial econometric techniques. To address data limitations, I invest in innovative measurement strategies, including remote sensing, deep learning applied to satellite imagery, network measures derived from mobile call detail records, automated web scraping, and the digitization of archival and ethnographic sources.

State Capacity and the Political Economy of Public-Service Provision

My paper, “The Social Bureaucrat: How Social Proximity among Bureaucrats Affects Local Governance” (forthcoming, American Journal of Political Science), develops a theory of bureaucratic efficiency that complements accountability-based explanations of service delivery with a capacity-based mechanism: efficiency increases with social proximity among bureaucrats, as informal ties reduce the coordination and transaction costs of public service provision. I test this argument using a spatial regression discontinuity design and novel data on rural water infrastructure across more than 35,000 villages in Turkey, combined with network measures constructed from antenna-level mobile call detail records and an original dataset mapping the ethno-religious distribution of villages. The paper has received the APSA Best Comparative Policy Paper Award, the MPSA Phi Beta Kappa Award, the MPSA Kenneth Meier Award for Best Paper in Public Policy and Administration, and the MPSA Best Paper by an Emerging Scholar Award, among others.

A companion paper with Fotini Christia (MIT), “Imams and Patrons: Service Provision by Islamist Non-State Actors” (forthcoming, Journal of Conflict Resolution), analyzes spatial and temporal variation in the service networks of an Islamist non-state actor across Turkey’s 970 districts, showing that non-state welfare provision depends critically on the movement’s ability to mobilize local economic elites rather than on strategic political calculation alone. A third paper, “Members of the Same Club?: Subnational Variations in Electoral Returns to Public Goods” (APSA McGillivray Honorable Mention), uses a generalized difference-in-differences design to show that electoral returns to local public goods increase with their perceived excludability, except where identity-based cleavages attenuate this relationship.

Extending this pillar to the enforcement dimension of state capacity, my co-authored paper with Alisha Holland (Harvard), “Enforcement Process Tracing: Forbearance and Dilution in Urban Colombia and Turkey” (Studies in Comparative International Development), shows that enforcement decisions regarding informal housing and businesses vary systematically over electoral cycles. Alongside this research, I was invited to author “Municipal Government” in Oxford Bibliographies in Urban Studies (Oxford University Press, 2025), which synthesizes the comparative literature on local government.

Colonial Extraction and Long-Run Effects on Development Outcomes

My second research pillar examines the long-term impact of extractive colonial interventions on political institutions, public service provision, and human development in sub-Saharan Africa. With Robin Harding (Oxford), Arinze Nwokolo (Lagos Business School), and Henrique Pita Barros (ISEG Lisbon), I am developing a coordinated set of projects examining this question. Our first paper, “Putting Property Rights in Context: Colonial Cotton Concessions, Customary Land Tenure, and Development in Mozambique,” draws on archival maps of colonial concession boundaries and implements a spatial regression discontinuity design to assess the long-run consequences of coercive agricultural production in Mozambique. Contrary to canonical theories in institutional economics associated with Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson, we find that areas subjected to extractive colonial rule exhibit worse long-run education- and health-related outcomes and weaker public service infrastructure, despite higher rates of private land and home ownership today: by eroding customary land authorities, extractive institutions weakened local collective action and diminished the resources available for communal service provision. Building on this work, my co-authors and I are preparing a large-scale Wellcome Trust and ERC application examining the long-term impact of extractive colonial interventions on political institutions, public service provision, and human development in sub-Saharan Africa. We are extending this framework to additional cases in sub-Saharan Africa to examine the impact of different types of extractive interventions on current political institutions, policy implementation, and development outcomes.

Refugee Mobility, Integration, and Climate Vulnerability

This pillar investigates how displaced populations integrate into host societies, including spatial mobility, segregation, and exposure to environmental risk, using panel designs. Two book chapters, in Guide to Mobile Data Analytics in Refugee Scenarios (Springer, 2019) and Data Science for Migration and Mobility (Oxford University Press, 2022), draw on geocoded and time-stamped mobile call detail records from more than 400,000 Syrian refugees and 1 million Turkish citizens to develop measures of spatial mobility and integration. They also show that combining mobile data with satellite imagery, and explicitly accounting for spatial distance, improves the measurement of refugees’ mobility and settlement patterns relative to conventional indicators aggregated at administrative levels.

A current working paper, “Employment Type and Refugees’ Climate Vulnerability: Evidence from Geocoded Mobility Patterns during Seasonal Agricultural Employment,” integrates geocoded mobile call detail records with high-resolution data on temperature extremes and precipitation, as well as processed geospatial land cover data (e.g., CORINE) to identify patterns of seasonal agricultural employment. It examines whether refugees engaged in seasonal work are disproportionately exposed to climate hazards relative to natives, with direct implications for refugee welfare and social protection policies in host countries across the Mediterranean and beyond.

The Political Economy of Disaster Resilience in Urban and Rural Governance

This stream represents a newly emerging research agenda that I am actively developing alongside the colonial-extraction pillar described above, and it constitutes one of the two core pillars underlying my ERC and UKRI Future Leaders Fellowship applications. It examines the political economy of disaster resilience, focusing on how privatized service delivery and political competition shape local vulnerability to natural disasters through the weak regulatory enforcement of private infrastructure and service providers. I measure resilience through variation in damage from natural disasters such as wildfires, floods, and earthquakes, drawing on cases severely affected by recent events, including Spain, Pakistan, Turkey, and California.

To construct comparable measures of damage where processed geospatial data are unavailable, I use deep-learning approaches applied to remote sensing and satellite imagery, an approach that is particularly valuable in Global South contexts where political and administrative constraints limit access to official data. My first working paper in this agenda, “Privatization without Regulation: The Political Economy of Electricity Infrastructure and Wildfires,” shows that the privatisation of electricity services leads to deteriorating infrastructure quality and higher wildfire incidence under weak regulatory enforcement. It also examines how this relationship varies across political contexts, testing whether electoral districts represented by opposition politicians are more resilient than their counterparts despite privatisation. I evaluate this argument using a difference-in-differences design that combines electricity outage data, electoral records, the NASA Global Fire Atlas, and geocoded wildfire incidents across Turkey.

Teaching Experience

My research and training equip me to teach across undergraduate and graduate programmes at DSPI and DPIR. On the methods side, I would contribute teaching in causal inference, statistics, econometrics, experimental methods, impact evaluation, and data science in R, and develop new offerings in advanced geospatial analysis and machine learning for policy applications. On the substantive side, I would offer and design courses on comparative politics, the political economy of development, comparative public policy, bureaucracy and the state, urban and local politics, the politics of disaster resilience, migration and displacement, and the politics of development in the Middle East.

At King’s, I have designed and convened three modules. My MA-level Quantitative Research Methods module pairs lectures on causal identification and econometrics with weekly hands-on R lab sessions and has received some of the highest student assessment scores. Following its success, the department expanded enrolment and introduced a similar undergraduate-level course into the core BA curriculum. My MA-level Political Economy of Development in Muslim Societies pairs comparative arguments with empirical applications from Muslim-majority contexts. I have also co-convened the BA core module Introduction to Politics (with over 100 students annually). I currently supervise approximately twelve BA and MA dissertations per year and co-supervise four doctoral students; all four have passed their upgrades without extension and are on track to complete on time.

I served as chair of quantitative methods roundtables for PhD students at the King’s School of Social Sciences and Public Policy Methods Centre. In May 2026, I will deliver the Advanced Geospatial Analysis workshop at the MZES Summer School for Women in Political Methodology at the University of Mannheim, and I have organised major methods events, including POLMETH Europe (2023).

My prior teaching includes graduate-level courses at MIT (Quantitative Methodology I: Regression (instructor: Daniel Hidalgo) and Machine Learning and Data Science in Politics (instructor: In Song Kim)), workshops in LaTeX, GitHub, and geospatial analysis at MIT’s Political Methodology Lab, and a teaching assistantship in J-PAL’s Executive Education course in causal inference for government and NGO practitioners.

Teaching Philosophy

Classroom inclusiveness. The graduate cohorts at DSPI and DPIR draw students from across the world and from fields as varied as computer science, economics, law, and public administration, and my experience teaching similarly heterogeneous cohorts has shaped how I design my modules. As a first-generation university student from Turkey who pursued graduate education across Europe and the United States, I am acutely aware of the barriers that students from varied starting points may face. In practice, I use anonymous online forums where reluctant students can raise questions, foster an inclusive discussion environment by emphasising that each student brings distinctive knowledge to the classroom, and share annotated tutorials and datasets through GitHub.

Mentorship. My approach to advising is to begin with students’ own research questions and help them assess the trade-offs between topics, methods, and data sources, rather than directing them toward a pre-set approach. I am ready to take on primary doctoral supervision at Oxford and am particularly committed to supporting students from underrepresented backgrounds, as reflected in my service on the APSA Committee on the Status of First Generation Scholars and the APSA Political Economy Section, as well as my fellowship with the Association for Analytic Learning about Islam and Muslim Societies.

Making technical material accessible. Teaching causal inference, data science, and quantitative methods to students with varied technical backgrounds requires care in design and delivery. I prioritise intuitive explanations and worked examples before introducing formal notation, and pair each methods lecture with a hands-on R lab session, consistently identified as the most valuable feature in student feedback. I prepare supplementary materials, tutorials, and annotated code and share them on GitHub and RPubs so they remain accessible after formal training ends.
